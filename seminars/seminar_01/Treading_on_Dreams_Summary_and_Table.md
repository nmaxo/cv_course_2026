# Treading on Dreams. Stories from Ireland

## Summary for presentation

**Treading on Dreams: Stories from Ireland** is a short story collection from the **Oxford Bookworms Library** (Stage 5), edited by **Clare West**, Oxford University Press (2007). The book is aimed at English language learners and contains adapted short stories by eight Irish writers.

### Authors in the collection
- Brian Friel  
- Edna O'Brien  
- William Trevor  
- Lorcan Byrne  
- Frank O'Connor  
- Claire Keegan  
- Eamonn Sweeney  
- Somerville and Ross (Edith Somerville and Violet Martin)

### Overall themes
Innocence and experience, loss and longing, humour and sadness. The opening evokes rural Ireland: "At home we started with an innocent life" — walking home from village dances across pale wet fields, birds on the moonlit lake, playing a tune across the water in the clear cold air.

### Culture-specific features of Irish prose in the collection
- **Setting**: Rural Ireland, villages, the West, counties (Donegal, Cork, Wicklow, Leitrim, Wexford, etc.).
- **Religion**: Catholicism, first confession and first communion, priests and nuns in upbringing.
- **Social life**: Village dances, ballrooms, pubs, hunting, horse races, neighbourly relations.
- **History and class**: Anglo-Irish gentry, Resident Magistrate (R.M.), relations between different social groups.
- **Tone**: Irony, understatement, small fates, gentle sad humour.
- **Family**: Grandmothers in the house, generational conflict, strict sisters or elders.

## Story retellings

### 1. Mr Sing My Heart's Delight (Brian Friel)

A young man, a poor travelling salesman, makes a long and difficult journey of about fifty miles by van, train, and on foot to reach his grandmother's house in a remote part of County Donegal. The house stands on top of a large rock at the end of the village, in the wild and beautiful northwest of Ireland. He has run away from his strict parents and his brothers and sisters at home, and he spends the whole winter with his grandmother, helping her and enjoying the peace of the place. She asks him simple, innocent questions about his life and his travels—whether he has ever been on a bus, whether it was bad on his stomach—and their conversations are full of warmth and gentle humour. The story contrasts his hard life on the road with the comfort and kindness he finds in this isolated spot and with his grandmother's affection. It is a story about escape, family, and the need to belong somewhere.

### 2. Irish Revel (Edna O'Brien)

Mary is seventeen and lives on a remote mountain farm in the west of Ireland. When she is invited to a party at the Commercial Hotel in the village below, she is thrilled. She cycles down with a flat tyre, wearing the family's best black lace dress (which she is not even allowed to wear to mass), and with her hair done in crimped waves. She is secretly hoping to see again John Roland, an English painter who stayed with her family two summers before and who has stayed in her imagination ever since. But as soon as she arrives, she is treated not as a guest but as unpaid help. The town girls mock her as a "mountainy" girl while she helps prepare the party. The guests are all men, and John Roland is not among them. As the evening goes on, the men get drunk and rowdy. The night ends in fear: Mary has to barricade herself behind furniture against the door to avoid being assaulted. Her romantic hopes are shattered, and she realises she would have been better off staying at home. The story shows the cruelty of small-town society and the gap between innocent dreams and harsh reality.

### 3. The Third Party (William Trevor)

Two men in their early forties meet by arrangement in the bar of Buswell's Hotel in Dublin. One is Boland, who owns a bakery; the other is Lairdman, who is having an affair with Boland's wife, Annabella. Lairdman wants to marry her and start a family and has come to ask Boland to agree to a divorce. Boland at first seems willing to let his wife go and even appears calm. They drink Jameson whiskey and discover they went to the same school—Boland as a boarder, Lairdman as a day boy. As the drink takes effect, Boland becomes nastier. He reminds Lairdman of an old humiliation when bullies pushed his head down a toilet. Then he drops the real blow: he tells Lairdman that Annabella is infertile—the childlessness was never Lairdman's fault, and she has let him think otherwise. Lairdman leaves in anger. Boland, driving home and stopping for more drinks on the way, understands that although he wanted his wife to leave, he has just made sure she will stay: he has sabotaged her chance of a new life and trapped all three of them in the same miserable, loveless situation. The story is about wounded pride, cruelty, and the way people can ruin what they claim to want.

### 5. My Oedipus Complex (Frank O'Connor)

Larry is five years old and lives with his mother in Ireland while his father is away at the war. For as long as he can remember, he has had his mother to himself: they talk in bed in the morning, go for walks to church and into town, and at night he says prayers for his father, who seems as remote as Santa Claus. When the war ends and his father comes home, everything changes. His father takes his place in the bed and expects his wife's attention. Larry is jealous and cross. On walks, his father is interested in talking to other men, not in the trams and ships that fascinate Larry. The two of them quarrel and compete for the mother's love. Then the baby brother, Sonny, is born. Suddenly the mother is absorbed in the new child. Larry finds that he and his father are in the same boat—both pushed to the side, both wanting her care. For the first time he feels something like sympathy for his father, and the two begin to get on. The story is told with humour from the child's point of view and is about jealousy, growing up, and learning to share the people we love.

### 6. Men and Women (Claire Keegan)

A young woman looks back on her teens on a farm in rural Ireland. She has a brother who is treated as "the brains" of the family and is excused from farm work so he can study. She resents this: she is the one who has to help her father with his buying and selling, even though his arthritis makes it hard for him to get about. She has to manage sheep in the back of the car and do jobs he cannot do himself. At the same time, she sees him at parties, dancing with other women without any trouble. Her mother seems to put up with it all and says little. The narrator is sharp and observant: she sees the unfairness in how boys and girls are raised and how her father and brother take more than their share of comfort and respect. The story builds to a moment when her mother at last speaks up and demands to be treated properly. It is a story about gender, family, and the small, everyday ways that power and unfairness work in a traditional Irish household.

### 7. Lord McDonald (Eamonn Sweeney)

Michael is one of the finest violinists in the world. He was born in Ireland but now lives in New York, in a small rented room in a crowded, built-up part of the city. The place feels tight and foreign to him; it is nothing like the open landscape he grew up in. The story explores his sense of displacement: he has achieved great success as a musician, but his new life does not feel like home. He is caught between his talent and his roots, between the city that has made his career and the country he still carries inside him. The story is about exile, identity, and the cost of leaving Ireland for a life abroad.

### 8. A Fishy Story (Somerville & Ross)

"A Fishy Story" comes from the Irish R.M. series by Somerville and Ross (the title in the original collection is "Poisson d'Avril," or "April Fish," linked to April Fools' Day). Major Sinclair Yeates is a Resident Magistrate—an Englishman who has taken a post in the west of Ireland, in West Cork. The stories describe his comic struggles to understand and deal with the local people and their ways. In this one, as in others, he is the straight man: things go wrong, plans backfire, and the locals (including his landlord and hunting companion, Flurry Knox) are full of schemes and surprises. The humour comes from the gap between the Major's orderly expectations and the chaos of Irish country life—hunting, horses, and village goings-on. The tone is light and satirical, poking fun at both the Anglo-Irish gentry and the misunderstandings between the magistrate and the Irish community around him.
